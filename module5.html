5.1

Legal and Ethical Issues

The same skills that make a good attacker make a good defender — the difference is what's legal.

5.1.1

Legal Issues in Cybersecurity

To protect against attacks, cybersecurity professionals need many of the same skills as attackers — the difference is that professionals use those skills within the bounds of the law.

👤

Personal Legal Issues

You may have the skills and opportunity to hack another person's computer or network, at work or at home — but 'just because you can does not mean you should.' Most hacks leave tracks that trace back to you. There is always demand for people who put cyber skills to good use within legal bounds.

🏢

Corporate Legal Issues

Businesses must abide by the cybersecurity laws of the countries they operate in. Breaking those laws on the job can cost you your job, or lead to prosecution, fines, or sentencing. When in doubt, assume an action is unlawful and check with your legal or HR department first.

International cybersecurity law is a constantly evolving field. Cyber attacks occur in cyberspace — an electronic space with no traditional geographic boundaries — which makes it far easier to mask the source of an attack than in conventional warfare. Country practice, opinio juris (a country's sense that it is legally bound), and treaties still under negotiation continue to shape how international law treats cyberspace.

5.1.2

Ethical Issues in Cybersecurity

Ethical Dilemma

A penetration test you carried out for @Apollo revealed that a colleague who started at the same time as you was responsible for a data breach.

You're thinking about leaving this out of your report so they don't get in trouble. What should you do?

Ask yourself these questions to decide on the best course of action:

  • Is it legal?
  • Does your action comply with company policy?
  • Will your action be favorable for the company and its stakeholders?
  • Would it be okay if everyone in the company took this action?
  • Would the outcome represent the company in a positive light in a news headline?

Many professional IT organizations — such as the Information Systems Security Association (ISSA) — publish Codes of Ethics to guide employee actions and behaviors. Cisco maintains a team devoted exclusively to ethical business conduct, and a Code of Business Conduct to help employees make decisions and resolve issues they encounter.

5.2

Education and Careers

Certifications worth pursuing, and where to look for your first role.

5.2.1

Become a Cybersecurity Guru

There are many cybersecurity jobs to consider beyond an entry-level Security Analyst role. Even at the start of your career, it's worth browsing job search engines — Indeed, LinkedIn, Monster, CareerBuilder — to get a sense of what's available worldwide.

5.2.2

Professional Certifications

Certifications are an excellent way to verify your skills and boost your career. Here are some of the most recognized, roughly in order of experience level:

1

Microsoft Technology Associate (MTA) Security Fundamentals

Aimed at high school and early college students, and those considering a career change.

2

Palo Alto Networks Certified Cybersecurity Associate

An entry-level certification for newcomers starting a cybersecurity career.

3

ISACA CSX Cybersecurity Fundamentals Certificate

Geared toward recent post-secondary graduates and career changers. Does not expire or require recertification.

4

CompTIA Security+

An entry-level certification meeting U.S. DoD Directive 8570.01-M — essential for federal IT security work.

5

EC-Council Certified Ethical Hacker (CEH)

Tests your ability to find weaknesses and vulnerabilities using the same tools as a malicious hacker — but lawfully and legitimately.

6

ISC2 CISSP

The most recognizable and popular security certification. Requires at least five years of relevant industry experience to sit the exam.

7

Cisco Certified CyberOps Associate

Validates the skills required of associate-level cybersecurity analysts working in security operations centers.

✎ Quick Check
You're a recent graduate with no prior industry experience, looking for your very first cybersecurity certification. Which is the better starting point?
Why: CISSP requires five years of industry experience, so it isn't accessible yet. The ISACA CSX Cybersecurity Fundamentals Certificate is aimed specifically at recent graduates and career changers, with no expiration or recertification requirement.
Module Recap

What You Learned

✓

Cybersecurity professionals need many of the same skills as attackers, but must use them within the bounds of the law.

✓

Personal legal issues arise when skills are misused, even informally; corporate legal issues arise when an organization fails to meet the cybersecurity laws of the countries it operates in.

✓

International cybersecurity law is still evolving, complicated by the borderless nature of cyberspace.

✓

Ethical decision-making means checking an action against legality, policy, stakeholder impact, and how it would look publicly.

✓

Professional bodies like ISSA publish Codes of Ethics, and organizations like Cisco maintain their own Code of Business Conduct.

✓

Certifications such as CompTIA Security+, CEH, CISSP, and Cisco Certified CyberOps Associate mark different stages of a cybersecurity career.

✓

Tools like CyberSeek help you understand supply and demand in the cybersecurity job market as you plan your next step.
