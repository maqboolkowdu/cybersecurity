3.1

Protecting Your Devices and Network

The basics that stop most attacks before they start.

3.1.2

Protecting Your Computing Devices

Tip Why It Matters
Turn the Firewall On Use at least one firewall — software or hardware — kept on and updated to prevent unauthorized access to your device.
Install Antivirus & Antispyware Malicious software can destroy data, slow your computer, or broadcast spam from your account. Only download software from trusted sites, and always run antivirus/antispyware to scan for and remove threats.
Manage Your OS and Browser Set security settings to medium or higher, and regularly install the latest patches and updates from vendors.
Set Up Password Protection Password-protect every device and encrypt sensitive or confidential data. Store only what you need on mobile devices — a compromised device can expose everything in your cloud storage too.
3.1.3

Wireless Network Security at Home

Wireless networks let Wi-Fi-enabled devices connect via an SSID. A router can be configured to hide the SSID, but that alone isn't enough security. Attackers know common default SSIDs and passwords — change both. You should also encrypt wireless communication using WPA2.

⚠
KRACK — 2017

Even with WPA2 enabled, a flaw discovered in the protocol allows Key Reinstallation Attacks (KRACKs), which break the encryption between a router and device to expose network data. This affects essentially all modern protected Wi-Fi networks.

To mitigate this, you should:

  • Update all Wi-Fi-capable devices as soon as security updates are available
  • Use a wired connection for any devices that support it
  • Use a trusted VPN service when accessing a wireless network
3.1.4

Public Wi-Fi Risks

Public wireless networks and Wi-Fi hotspots let you get online away from home — but come with risk, so it's best not to send personal information over them. Continuously verify that your device does not have file and media sharing enabled and that it requires authenticated, encrypted connections.

Use an encrypted VPN service to prevent 'eavesdropping' over public Wi-Fi — it encrypts the connection between your device and the VPN server, so even if attackers intercept a data transmission, they cannot decipher it.

3.1.6

Building a Strong Password

🖼
Visual reference

Original slide includes a graphic of password-strength tips — summarized below.

  • Don't use dictionary words or letter/number sequences in any language
  • Don't repeat common words spelled backward, common misspellings, or abbreviations
  • Don't use identifying information: birthdays, names, or pet names
  • Don't reuse the same password across multiple sites
  • Use complex combinations of uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and special characters
3.1.7

Using a Passphrase

Consider passphrases instead of passwords to prevent unauthorized access. A passphrase usually takes the form of a sentence — for example, 'Acat th@tlov3sd0gs.' — making it easier to remember. Because it's longer than a typical password, it's also less vulnerable to dictionary or brute-force attacks.

  • Choose a memorable sentence
  • Add complexity: mix in cases, numbers, and symbols
  • The longer the better — length matters more than complexity alone
  • Avoid common or famous phrases, such as song lyrics
3.1.8

NIST Password Guidelines

The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) has published improved password requirements. While intended for government applications, they serve as a solid standard for any sector — aiming to place the burden of verification on service providers rather than users.

  • Passwords should be at least 8 characters but no more than 64
  • Commonly guessed passwords like 'password' or 'abc123' should be blocked
  • No composition rules requiring specific mixes of cases and numbers
  • Users should be able to see the password while typing, to reduce errors
  • All printing characters and spaces should be allowed
  • There should be no password hints
  • There should be no forced password expiration period
  • There should be no knowledge-based authentication (secret questions, transaction history)
3.2

Data Maintenance

Encryption, backups, and making sure deleted really means deleted.

3.2.1

What Is Encryption?

Encryption is the process of converting information into a form that unauthorized parties cannot read. Only a trusted, authorized person with a secret key or password can decrypt the data back into its original form.

⚠
Important distinction

Encryption does not prevent someone from intercepting the data — it only prevents an unauthorized person from viewing or accessing the content. Some criminals will encrypt your data and hold it for ransom.

3.2.2

How Do You Encrypt Your Data?

Software programs can encrypt files, folders, or entire drives. EFS (Encrypting File System) is a Windows feature linked to a specific user account — only the user who encrypted the data can access it afterward.

1

Select the data

Choose one or more files or folders.

2

Open Properties

Right-click the selected data and go to 'Properties.'

3

Find Advanced

Click 'Advanced' in the Properties dialog.

4

Enable encryption

Select the 'Encrypt contents to secure data' checkbox.

5

Confirm it worked

Files and folders encrypted with EFS are displayed in green.

3.2.3

Back Up Your Data

A backup can prevent the loss of irreplaceable data. To back up correctly, you need an additional storage location and must copy data to it regularly.

Storage Option Trade-offs
Home Network Storing data locally means total control — but also total responsibility if something fails.
Secondary Location An external hard drive, NAS, thumb drives, or discs. You own and maintain the equipment and bear the cost.
The Cloud A subscription service (e.g. AWS) priced by storage space. Access depends on your account, but data survives device failure, fire, or theft.
3.2.5

Deleting Data Permanently

Have you ever deleted data or disposed of a hard drive without a second thought? To erase data so it's no longer recoverable, it must be overwritten with ones and zeros multiple times, using purpose-built tools.

  • SDelete (Microsoft) claims to remove sensitive files completely
  • Shred (Linux) and Secure Empty Trash (macOS) offer similar services
  • The only guaranteed method is to physically destroy the hard drive or storage device
⚠
Reality check

Many criminals have taken advantage of files that their previous owners assumed were impenetrable or unrecoverable.

Who Owns Your Data?

The fine print you agree to every time you tap 'Accept.'

3.3.2

Understand the Terms

3.3.3

What Are You Agreeing To?

Scenario · @Apollo Photo Sharing

You've just created an account with an online photo-sharing company and agreed to its Terms of Service.

But do you really know what you signed up for?

3.3.6

Before You Sign Up

What factors should you consider before signing up for an online service?

  • Have you read the Terms of Service?
  • What are your rights regarding your data?
  • Can you request a copy of your data?
  • What can the provider do with the data you upload?
  • What happens to your information when you close your account?
3.4

Safeguarding Your Online Privacy

Layer your defenses — extra login factors, careful sharing, and private browsing.

3.4.1

Two-Factor Authentication

Popular services like Google, Facebook, Twitter, LinkedIn, Apple, and Microsoft use two-factor authentication to add an extra layer of security beyond your username, password, or PIN. The second factor is usually one of:

💳

A Physical Object

A credit card, mobile phone, or hardware fob.

🫆

A Biometric Scan

A fingerprint, or facial or voice recognition.

🔢

A Verification Code

Sent via SMS or email at login time.

3.4.2

Open Authorization (OAuth)

Open Authorization (OAuth) is an open standard protocol that lets you use existing credentials to access third-party applications without exposing your password.

In practice

You want to register for Cisco's next course in this series, but you need to log into the eLearning portal first — and you can't remember your login details.

No problem: the portal lets you sign in using your credentials from a social media account, such as Facebook or Google.

Instead of resetting your login, you sign in instantly with your existing account and register for the course.

3.4.4

Email and Web Browser Privacy

⚠
Don't Get Spoofed

A simple forged or spoofed email can lead to a massive data breach and cause irreversible damage to your reputation.

These risks can be minimized by enabling private browsing mode. Every major browser has its own name for it:

Browser Private Mode Name
Microsoft Internet Explorer InPrivate
Google Chrome Incognito
Mozilla Firefox Private tab / private window
Safari Private Browsing

When private mode is enabled, cookies are disabled, temporary internet files are removed, and browsing history clears when you close the window. This can help prevent others from gathering data about your activity and targeting you with ads — though companies keep developing new fingerprinting techniques even against private browsing.
