2.1

Analyzing a Cyber Attack

The malware family tree, and the warning signs that your system is already infected.

2.1.1

Types of Malware

Cybercriminals use many different types of malicious software — malware — to carry out their activities. Malware is any code that can steal data, bypass access controls, or cause harm to or compromise a system. Knowing the different types and how they spread is key to containing and removing them.

👁

Spyware

Monitors your online activity, can log every keystroke, and captures sensitive data such as online banking details by modifying device security settings. Often bundles with legitimate software or Trojan horses.

📢

Adware

Automatically delivers advertisements to a user, most often via a web browser — hard to ignore with constant pop-ups. Commonly bundled with spyware.

🚪

Backdoor

Gains unauthorized access by bypassing normal authentication, letting hackers access resources and issue remote system commands. Works quietly in the background.

🔒

Ransomware

Holds a computer system or its data captive — usually by encryption — until a payment is made. Often spread through phishing emails or software vulnerabilities.

⚠

Scareware

Uses scare tactics — fake OS-style warnings that your system is at risk — to trick you into running a program that then infects your system.

🕳

Rootkit

Modifies the operating system to create a backdoor for remote access, often via privilege escalation. Can tamper with forensics tools, making it very hard to detect.

🦠

Viruses

Replicates and attaches itself to executable files when run, usually needing user interaction to activate. Can be harmless or destructive, and spreads via USB drives, discs, network shares, or email.

🎁

Trojan Horse

Masks its true intent, appearing legitimate while hiding inside image, audio, or game files. Unlike viruses, it doesn't self-replicate — it relies on tricking the user.

🪱

Worms

Replicates and spreads on its own — no host program required. Exploits vulnerabilities to propagate rapidly; the 2001 Code Red worm infected over 300,000 servers in just 19 hours.

2.1.2

Symptoms of Malware

Regardless of the type of malware infecting a system, watch for these common symptoms:

  • An increase in CPU usage that slows down your device
  • Your computer freezing or crashing often
  • A decrease in your web browsing speed
  • Unexplainable problems with your network connections
  • Modified or deleted files
  • The presence of unknown files, programs, or desktop icons
  • Unknown processes running
  • Programs turning off or reconfiguring themselves
  • Sending emails without your knowledge or consent
✎ Quick Check
A machine has become noticeably slower, browser pop-ups appear constantly, and an unfamiliar toolbar has installed itself. Which malware type best fits these symptoms?
Why: Constant pop-up advertisements and unwanted browser add-ons are the classic signature of adware, which is designed to automatically deliver ads to a user.
2.2

Methods of Infiltration

How attackers actually get in — from tricking people to cracking passwords to flooding networks.

2.2.1

Social Engineering

Social engineering is manipulating people into performing actions or divulging confidential information. Social engineers rely on people's willingness to help, but also prey on their weaknesses — for example, calling an authorized employee with an 'urgent' problem and appealing to vanity, greed, or authority to gain network access.

☎

Pretexting

An attacker calls an individual and lies to gain access to confidential data — e.g. pretending to need personal or financial details to 'confirm identity.'

🚶

Tailgating

An attacker quickly follows an authorized person into a secure, physical location.

🎁

Quid Pro Quo

An attacker requests personal information from someone in exchange for something, like a small gift.

2.2.2

Denial-of-Service (DoS) & Distributed DoS

A Denial-of-Service (DoS) attack is relatively simple to carry out, even for an unskilled attacker, and results in some interruption of network service to users, devices, or applications.

🌊

Overwhelming Traffic

A network, host, or application is sent an enormous amount of data at a rate it can't handle, causing slow response or a crash.

📦

Maliciously Formatted Packets

An attacker sends packets containing errors or improper formatting that the receiving application can't handle, slowing or crashing the device.

A Distributed DoS (DDoS) attack is similar but originates from multiple, coordinated sources. An attacker builds a network — a botnet — of infected 'zombie' hosts controlled by handler systems. Zombies continually scan and infect more hosts; when ready, the attacker instructs the handlers to launch the DDoS attack together.

2.2.4

Botnets

A bot computer is typically infected by visiting an unsafe website or opening an infected email attachment or media file. A botnet is a group of bots connected through the internet that a malicious individual or group can control — sometimes tens or hundreds of thousands of them, run through a command-and-control server.

Activating bots can distribute malware, launch DDoS attacks, send spam, or run brute-force password attacks. Criminals often rent botnets to third parties. Organizations like Cisco force network traffic through botnet filters that detect traffic from infected devices and push updated filters via cloud-based threat intelligence, alerting internal security teams to remediate.

2.2.5

On-Path Attacks

On-path attackers intercept or modify communications between two devices — such as a web browser and a web server — to collect information from, or impersonate, one of them.

MitM

Man-in-the-Middle

A criminal takes control of a device without the user's knowledge, intercepting and capturing information — often financial — before it reaches its destination.

MitMo

Man-in-the-Mobile

A variation of MitM that targets mobile devices, exfiltrating sensitive information. ZeuS, for example, quietly captures two-step verification SMS codes.

2.2.6

SEO Poisoning

Search Engine Optimization (SEO) is about improving a website's visibility in search results based on content relevance. Attackers abuse popular search terms to push malicious sites higher in the rankings — a technique called SEO poisoning — most often to drive traffic to sites hosting malware or social engineering attempts.

2.2.8

Password Attacks

Entering a username and password is one of the most popular ways to authenticate — which makes uncovering your password an easy win for criminals.

▦

Password Spraying

Sprays a few commonly used passwords (e.g. 'Password123') across many accounts before trying the next common password — avoiding lockouts and detection.

📖

Dictionary Attack

Systematically tries every word in a dictionary, or a list of commonly used words, as a password.

💪

Brute-Force Attack

The simplest and most common method — tries every possible combination of letters, numbers, and symbols until it finds the right one.

🌈

Rainbow Attack

Passwords are stored as hashed values, not plain text. A rainbow table is a precomputed dictionary of hashes; matching a stolen hash reveals the password instantly.

⚠
Traffic Interception

If you store or send a password in plain, readable text, anyone with access to your account, device, or the network path — authorized or not — can simply read it.

2.2.10

Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs)

An Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) is a multi-phase, long-term, stealthy, and advanced operation against a specific target. Individual attackers usually lack the skill, resources, or persistence to pull one off — APTs are typically well-funded and target organizations or nations for business or political reasons. The goal is to deploy customized malware and remain undetected on the target's systems.

2.3

Security Vulnerability and Exploits

Where weaknesses hide — in the chips themselves, in code, and in how we manage both.

2.3.1

Hardware Vulnerabilities — Meltdown and Spectre

Hardware vulnerabilities are most often the result of design flaws. RAM, for example, is made of many capacitors installed very close together — and repeatedly 'hammering' one row of memory (the Rowhammer exploit) can trigger electrical interference that corrupts data in neighboring rows.

⚠
Meltdown & Spectre

Google security researchers discovered these two hardware vulnerabilities affecting nearly all CPUs released since 1995 — across desktops, laptops, servers, smartphones, and cloud services. Meltdown lets an attacker read all memory on a system; Spectre lets it read data handled by other applications, via side-channel attacks that run repeatedly with minimal risk of a crash.

2.3.2

Software Vulnerabilities

Errors in operating system or application code usually introduce software vulnerabilities. The SYNful Knock vulnerability, for example, let attackers gain control of enterprise-grade routers such as legacy Cisco ISR routers — monitoring all network communication and infecting other devices — by installing an altered IOS image. Always verify the integrity of downloaded firmware and restrict physical access to networking equipment.

2.3.3

Categorizing Software Vulnerabilities

▦

Buffer Overflow

Writing data beyond a memory buffer's limits lets an application reach memory allocated to other processes — leading to a crash, data compromise, or privilege escalation.

❓

Non-Validated Input

Programs that trust input without checking it can be manipulated — e.g. a maliciously crafted image with invalid dimensions can force incorrect buffer allocation.

⏱

Race Conditions

Occur when the outcome of an event depends on events happening in a specific order or timing, and that order isn't guaranteed.

🔑

Weak Security Practices

Rolling your own authentication, authorization, or encryption instead of using tested, proven libraries usually introduces new vulnerabilities.

🚧

Access Control Problems

Poorly managed access — physical or digital — undermines everything else. With physical access, an attacker can often bypass permissions entirely and read data straight off the disk.

2.3.4

Software Updates

The goal of software updates is to stay current and avoid exploitable vulnerabilities. Vendors like Microsoft and Apple release patches daily. Even with heavy investment in finding and patching vulnerabilities, new ones surface regularly — which is why many organizations hire third-party security researchers or run their own penetration testing teams.

i
Example

Google's Project Zero is a permanent team dedicated entirely to finding software vulnerabilities before attackers do, after Google discovered several in widely used end-user software.

2.4

The Cybersecurity Landscape

Cryptocurrency introduced a new asset worth stealing — and a new way to steal computing power itself.

2.4.1

Cryptocurrency

Cryptocurrency is digital money used to buy goods and services, secured with strong encryption techniques. Banks, governments, and companies alike are increasingly paying attention to it. Owners keep their money in encrypted, virtual 'wallets'; transactions between wallets are recorded on a decentralized, electronic ledger — a blockchain — carried out anonymously with no interference from central banks or governments.

Special computers collect data about the latest transactions every ten minutes, turning them into mathematical puzzles to preserve confidentiality. Verifying these transactions — mining — involves an army of high-end PCs solving puzzles to authenticate them. Once verified, the ledger updates and is copied worldwide across the blockchain network.

2.4.2

Cryptojacking

⚠
Emerging Threat

Cryptojacking hides on a user's computer, phone, tablet, or server, using that device's resources to 'mine' cryptocurrency without the owner's consent or knowledge. Many victims didn't even realize they'd been hacked until it was too late.

What You Learned

✓

Malware is any code that can steal data, bypass access controls, or cause harm to or compromise a system — spyware, adware, backdoors, ransomware, scareware, rootkits, viruses, Trojans, and worms all take different approaches.

✓

Common malware symptoms include high CPU usage, freezing or crashing, slow browsing, unexplained network issues, modified or deleted files, and unknown processes or programs.

✓

Social engineering manipulates people into performing actions or divulging confidential information by exploiting their willingness to help.

✓

A DoS attack interrupts network service; a DDoS attack does the same from multiple coordinated (botnet) sources at once.

✓

A botnet is a group of internet-connected bots controlled by an attacker, often rented out for malicious purposes.

✓

On-path attackers intercept or modify communications between two devices to steal information or impersonate one of them.

✓

Password attacks range from spraying and dictionary attacks to brute-force and rainbow-table attacks against stolen password hashes.

✓

Hardware vulnerabilities like Meltdown and Spectre stem from chip design flaws; software vulnerabilities stem from coding errors such as buffer overflows and non-validated input.

✓

Staying current with software updates is one of the simplest, highest-value defenses against known vulnerabilities.

✓

Cryptocurrency's rise introduced cryptojacking — silently using a victim's device to mine currency without consent.
